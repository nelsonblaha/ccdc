A proposal · El Paso · Ciudad Juárez

A data center that belongs to El Chuco.

Meta is building a $10 billion AI data center in Northeast El Paso. This is a proposal for an alternative that belongs to the people who live here, governed by a board its members elect.

Nothing is running yet. Signing up gets you occasional email about the project and nothing else.

We're not here to keep Meta honest, but to build something different that doesn't ask you to take its word.

We looked into it, and it turns out ethical AI services are easy to build as a non-profit, and much of what's needed is already ready and actively maintained by the global open source community. We also realized it doesn't have to be perfect from day one to be more promising than what we have now.

El Paso gets more than 300 sunny days a year. Meta's plan is 813 gas generators.

In October 2025 Meta announced a $1.5 billion AI data center in Northeast El Paso,[1] later expanded to a $10 billion project.[2] The company committed publicly to matching 100% of the facility's electricity with clean and renewable energy, including on-site solar.[5]

El Paso Electric's regulatory filings tell a different story about how the power actually arrives. The filings say large-scale solar and battery storage could theoretically supply the data center, but that such an installation would require thousands of acres adjacent to the site which are not available.[2] The utility has instead sought a noncompetitive contract to stack 813 modular natural gas generators to power it.[3][4]

“The company has made public commitments to power their project with renewable energy sources including on-site solar generation, and I think they need to be held to those commitments.”

Chris Canales, El Paso City Council[3]

Water

Meta's supply agreement with El Paso Water permits an average of 750,000 gallons of potable water per day, rising to a permitted average of 1.5 million gallons per day once the campus is fully built out. That is more than the Marathon Petroleum refinery in South-Central El Paso bought from El Paso Water last year.[2]

In fairness, and because getting this right matters: Meta says the facility uses a closed-loop, liquid-cooled system that recirculates the same water, will use zero water for the majority of the year, and will consume water at a rate comparable to a typical West Texas golf course.[5] Those are the company's figures. The permitted maximums above are what the agreement allows.

Everything you type into an AI assistant is a document about you, held by someone else.

In practice these are not conversations, they are records. Commercial assistants retain what you send, and several of them use it to train future models unless you find the setting that says otherwise. Some route a sample to humans for review. All of it is discoverable by subpoena. This is not a scandal; it is the normal design of a product whose economics require your data to be useful to the company.

People type things into these tools that they would not put in an email: the health question before the doctor's appointment, the immigration question, the letter to a landlord, the business idea. Treating that as ordinary telemetry is a choice, and it is one a member-governed organization has no reason to make.

What we would commit to

  • Your conversations are never used to train anything. Ours or anyone else's.
  • A stated retention period, with deletion that actually deletes, and a delete button that works.
  • No trackers, no advertising cookies, no analytics of our own. Not as a policy to be revisited; as a thing that isn't built.
  • We name the model provider and link their terms, so you can check what we claim against what they promise.

And the reason to believe it is structural rather than sentimental: these commitments sit in the founding documents at the highest amendment threshold, so changing one takes a supermajority of directors and leaves a permanent public record. The mechanism is written down before there is anything to protect.

The limit, stated plainly

At the start we rent computing power from a large cloud provider, so your text passes through their infrastructure under their contract. We can bind ourselves completely and we can only bind them by agreement. Anyone claiming otherwise while renting is overselling it.

What we can do is publish which provider, on which terms, and change providers if those terms change.

Who should own AI?

Ten billion dollars builds a data center owned by shareholders. The budget below builds a year of one owned by the people who use it. Those aren't competing bids for the same job. They are answers to different questions, and only one of them is being asked out loud right now.

When you pay $20 a month for an assistant, that money leaves the city and accrues to whoever holds the equity. When you pay $5 here, it pays for somebody else's free access, and the surplus stays in the valley. Same transaction, opposite direction.

The part that actually distinguishes this, though, isn't the price. There is no equity to hold. A nonprofit has no owners, so there is nobody to buy out, no acquisition, no exit, no investor who eventually needs a return larger than the community can pay. If it dissolves, the founding documents send whatever is left to another nonprofit, never to a founder and never to a member.

None of this requires believing anything about billionaires. It only requires noticing that infrastructure the public depends on tends to end up owned by whoever can afford to buy it, and that the exception is when people organize before that happens. Electric co-ops, credit unions, and mutual water companies all exist for that reason.

People have been self-hosting for twenty years. AI is the first thing that made chipping in together worth the trouble.

There is already a large movement of people running their own software instead of renting it from five companies. Nextcloud instead of Google Drive, Mastodon instead of X, Matrix instead of Discord, Jellyfin instead of Netflix, Immich instead of Google Photos. It works. It's mature. Millions of people do it.

Going in on a shared server together was never quite worth the hassle. Any one of those services runs happily on a cheap virtual machine or an old desktop in a closet. Once you add the cost of organizing, pooling money cost more than it saved. Someone has to administer it, someone has to pay, and someone has to answer for it when it breaks. So mostly people didn't.

AI is the first self-hosted service where the hardware is genuinely expensive and genuinely lumpy. You cannot run it on a Raspberry Pi in a closet. For the first time, pooling is obviously worth it.

Once the hard thing exists, every additional service costs almost nothing to add. The machine, the organization, the board, the billing and the person on call are all already in place. The expensive part was never the software. It was the trust and the coordination, and those are one-time costs.

This has been done before

The closest ancestor is older than the internet: rural electric cooperatives, formed across Texas because the utilities would not serve people the market found inconvenient, and still member-owned today. Credit unions did the same for banking. Both are ordinary, both are member-governed, and both were once described as impractical. Smaller versions of this exist for hosting too, and the ones worth reading about are in the research.

None of this is a novel idea. What would be new is doing it here, in El Paso and Juárez, on purpose, in the middle of an argument about who gets to build data centers in this valley.

The proposal

Nonprofit and accountable.

This is not a rival to Meta's facility, but something completely different: the same sort of AI assistant people pay $20 a month for, covering chat, document analysis, research and writing help, run by a Texas nonprofit whose board is elected by its members and whose books are open.

The reason a small nonprofit can offer that at all is that the hard part is done. The chat interface would be Open WebUI, a mature open-source project used by organisations far larger than this one. Documents, web search, voice and per-user permissions are all already built and freely licensed. We would be configuring it, not writing it.

To be clear about what this is not: it isn't a city program, it isn't asking for public money, and it has no connection to city government. It's a private nonprofit, joined and run by the people who live here, closer to a credit union or an electric co-op than to anything at City Hall.

A few things are simply not allowed. Your conversations are never used to train anything, not by us and not by whoever we rent computing capacity from. Other things are required: the finances and decisions published in full, and the electricity and water behind the service reported honestly.

How access would work

Free

$0, a real allowance rather than a trial

A minimum level of access for anyone living in the metro with an El Paso or Juárez address, on either side of the river. This is the point of the organization, not a marketing funnel.

Member

Flat annual dues, waived on request

Buys a vote for the board and a seat at the annual meeting. It does not buy compute. You don't have to pay for a usage plan to vote, or vote to pay for a plan.

Supporter

$5–50/month

More capacity, priced under the commercial alternative rather than against it. Pick what you can afford; the surplus pays for everyone on the free tier.

Hypothetical: a founding board would set the desired structure.

Roadmap

Act I. Exist

Now
01

Measure interest

Now

A list of people here who would use it. If that list stays short, this shouldn't exist. Add your name.

02

Recruit three founding directors

Now

Texas requires at least three. The board is the product here. People are being asked to trust this because of who sits on it. At least one should be someone willing to vote against the founder in public.

03

File the certificate of formation

Next

Form 202 with the Texas Secretary of State, $25. It names the purpose, whether there are voting members, and where the assets go if the organization ever dissolves. Those three lines are the mission lock, and they're hard to change later on purpose.

04

Adopt bylaws that bind us

Next

A supermajority of the membership, not just the board, is required to change the core commitments. A date certain for the first member election. No permanent founder seat.

Act II. Serve

Year One
05

Find a fiscal sponsor

Bridge

An existing charity that can accept tax-deductible donations on the project's behalf while the IRS application is pending. It also means someone else's bookkeeper is watching the money from day one.

06

Turn the service on

Build

One server running Open WebUI, free access for residents on both sides of the river, paid tiers below cost.

07

Become tax-exempt

Paperwork

IRS Form 1023, and the long form rather than the short one, because the short one is an attestation nobody checks and we'd rather be checked. Then Texas franchise and sales tax exemption, the nonprofit payment-processing rate, and Google's nonprofit cloud credits.

08

Hand over the keys

The point

First member election. Board seated by vote rather than by appointment. Any special founder role expires on the schedule written in step 04. First annual report and tax return published the day they're filed.

Act III. Grow

Future
09

One machine, many services

Members decide

Files, chat, photos, video calls, email for local nonprofits. The machine and the organization already exist by this point; adding a service is nearly free. Members vote on what's worth running and in what order.

10

Decide out loud what we won't do

Ongoing

Which AI models we're willing to buy and from whom. How long conversations are kept. Whose money we take. What we refuse to host. These are genuinely hard and reasonable people disagree, so the deliberation gets published, not just the verdict.

11

Own the hardware, here

The name

Local machines in El Paso, or wherever we determine is best, with a published water and power budget, sized honestly and reported against every year, including the years we miss. Until then we rent, and we say so.

12

Find allies and build something bigger

The horizon

One city's nonprofit can't out-build a hyperscaler. A network of them, pooling purchasing and operations with cooperative hosts, municipal broadband projects, and other community data centers, can start to make the case that data centers could be built better, and demonstrate it rather than demand it.

Honestly

The name says data center, and on day one this would not own one. Running our own AI hardware costs more and delivers worse results than buying capacity until there are thousands of members. At the start, this would be a nonprofit renting from the most responsible existing infrastructure we can find, under local governance and with the receipts published.

Why here

AI is global, governance is local.

Why build something for one metro instead of something national?

Because accountability does not survive being scaled up. A membership spread across a continent has no meeting it can actually convene, no director anyone runs into at the grocery store, and no water figure that means anything to the people voting on it. The remedy for governance you cannot check is not more governance at a greater distance. Every part of this that makes it trustworthy needs the organization to be small enough to be watched by the people it belongs to. An annual meeting you can attend, a board you can corner, a utility bill you can compare against your own.

So the problem is global and the governance has to be near. That is why step 12 is a network of local organizations rather than one big one: it is the only way this grows without becoming the same kind of remote institution it exists to answer.

El Chuco is special

  • It's one community. Families, businesses and daily routines straddle the border, and more than ten thousand people cross it on foot every day. That matters in practice. A membership organization can hold a meeting here, recruit a board, and be influenced face to face.
  • Geographic isolation. It is roughly 230 miles to Albuquerque and 300 to Tucson. This metro is nobody's suburb: its own media market, its own labor market, its own politics. Institutions that serve it have to be built here.
  • Shared experience. Most places would be learning the vocabulary from scratch. Water supply agreements, load growth, noncompetitive procurement. This one has spent a year on it, in council chambers and the local press. That is an unusual amount of civic expertise to have lying around, and it expires if nobody uses it.

The water doesn't stop at the bridge, so neither should the response

El Paso and Ciudad Juárez are one metropolitan area of roughly 2.5 million people that happens to have an international border running through it. More to the point here: the Rio Grande and the Hueco Bolson aquifer are shared, and so is the air. A data center's draw on either one is a binational fact whether or not anyone treats it that way.

What that costs us

Serving Juárez residents over the internet is genuinely straightforward. A US nonprofit may serve people in another country; charitable status does not require a domestic-only beneficiary class, and donations stay deductible because that depends on where the organization is, not where it works. The added work is a Spanish-language privacy notice that satisfies Mexican law, honoring data rights under it, and some extra reporting on the annual tax return.

Putting hardware or staff physically in Juárez is a different order of difficulty. A Mexican entity, its own tax registration, import duties on equipment, two sets of books. That is a later decision and we are not pretending otherwise.

Mexico replaced its federal data protection law in March 2025 and dissolved INAI, its independent privacy regulator, moving enforcement to a federal ministry. We do not yet know whether that law reaches a service run from servers in the United States. That question needs a Mexican lawyer, and it is on the list rather than quietly assumed away.
